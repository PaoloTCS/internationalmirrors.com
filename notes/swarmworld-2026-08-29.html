SwarmWorld and the defensive problem of technological inheritance

International Mirrors Reading note 001 Public research framing
On this page The result The adjacency Research agenda Disclosure boundary

SwarmWorld is strong evidence that persistent environments can support technological culture among language-model agents. International Mirrors focuses on the safety question created by that success: how a society tests what it inherits before inherited capability becomes inherited authority.

What SwarmWorld establishes

Pal, Wang, and Buehler place initially homogeneous language-model agents in a shared spatial world. Agents explore, process resources, construct durable artifacts, and write executable controllers. Those controllers are evaluated by a deterministic simulator under disturbances that the agents do not see in advance.

The important design separation is between cognition and consequence: agents propose; a fixed world determines what their proposals actually do. Shared societies develop broader and more resilient technological portfolios than an isolated-search baseline, while the strongest single artifact can still come from isolated search. Technologies persist through construction, executable inheritance, and agent–artifact networks. The paper reports that most reuse begins through physical observation rather than direct communication.

The central implication

In a society of agents, the environment is not background. It is memory, medium, curriculum, and executable inheritance at once.

The adjacent defensive question

SwarmWorld asks how technological culture can emerge. International Mirrors asks how such a culture can determine whether a persistent inheritance is sound, misattributed, corrupted, or dangerous in composition. This is a complementary problem, not a claim to reproduce or supersede the SwarmWorld result.

A research agenda for integrity

1. Provenance that survives transformation

Attribution must cover more than authorship. An artifact may be observed, compressed, translated, recombined, repaired, and executed by different agents. The relevant unit is the causal lineage from observation to consequence.

2. Latent composition

Components that appear acceptable in isolation may create a different capability when assembled. Integrity therefore cannot be reduced to component-level approval; the composition and the context of execution must also be tested.

3. Stigmergic infection

If agents learn from what prior agents leave behind, a persistent artifact can shape later behavior without any direct message. Experiments should measure how quickly a planted pattern propagates, mutates, becomes conventional, and resists correction.

4. Containment before authority

Evaluation should occur inside a sealed simulation with instrumented ground truth, constrained actions, and explicit escape boundaries. A candidate inheritance earns authority only after its observed consequences remain consistent with governing intent across relevant disturbances.

FIL/SARAI and the disclosure boundary

FIL/SARAI frames the problem as integrity across a chain of transformations: governing intent, observation, interpretation, composition, execution, and consequence. At this stage, International Mirrors is publishing the research questions, conceptual boundaries, and evaluation commitments. Proprietary or unfiled mechanisms are kept abstract. Future notes can make claims only at the level supported by reproducible, bounded experiments.

Source: Subhadeep Pal, Fiona Y. Wang, and Markus J. Buehler, SwarmWorld: Stigmergic technological evolution in societies of language-model agents, arXiv:2608.26081, submitted 26 August 2026.
